## Clarify first
- What operations can be performed on $n$? In one step, you can subtract any non-zero digit present in the decimal representation of the current number.
- What is the termination condition? The process stops as soon as $n$ reaches 0.
- What is the objective? Find the minimum number of subtraction steps required to reach 0.
- What are the constraints on $n$? $1 \le n \le 10^6$.

## Examples to say out loud
- Consider $n = 27$:
  - Digits of 27 are 2 and 7.
  - Subtract largest digit 7: $27 - 7 = 20$ (step 1).
  - Digits of 20 are 2 and 0 (non-zero is 2).
  - Subtract 2: $20 - 2 = 18$ (step 2).
  - Digits of 18 are 1 and 8. Subtract 8: $18 - 8 = 10$ (step 3).
  - Digits of 10 are 1 and 0. Subtract 1: $10 - 1 = 9$ (step 4).
  - Digits of 9 is 9. Subtract 9: $9 - 9 = 0$ (step 5).
  - Total steps = 5.
- Edge case: single digit $n = 9 \implies 9 - 9 = 0$ in 1 step.
- Edge case: $n = 10 \implies 10 - 1 = 9 \to 9 - 9 = 0$ in 2 steps.

## Brute force
A brute-force recursive search would try subtracting every distinct non-zero digit present in $n$ and branch into subproblems. For $n = 10^6$, exploring all branches without memoization causes exponential tree explosion. Dynamic programming with $\text{dp}[x] = 1 + \min_{d \in \text{digits}(x)} \text{dp}[x - d]$ computes the answer in $O(n \log_{10} n)$ time and $O(n)$ space. However, a greedy strategy that always subtracts the **maximum digit** is provably optimal, requiring only $O(\text{steps} \times \log_{10} n)$ time and $O(1)$ space.

## The key observation
Why is subtracting the largest digit always optimal?
At each step, subtracting a larger digit reduces the number as quickly as possible. Since smaller target numbers generally require fewer or equal steps to reach 0, and the set of available digits changes only as place-values decrease, jumping down by the maximum digit $\max(d)$ never deprives us of a faster sequence. Thus, a greedy choice coincides with the global minimum, reducing memory consumption to $O(1)$.

## Optimal approach
1. Initialize `steps = 0`.
2. While $n > 0$:
   - Find the maximum digit $\text{maxD}$ among the decimal digits of $n$.
   - Subtract $\text{maxD}$ from $n$: $n = n - \text{maxD}$.
   - Increment `steps`.
3. Return `steps`.

Complexity:
- Time: $O(\text{steps} \times \log_{10} n)$. In each step, extracting digits takes at most 7 division operations ($\log_{10}(10^6) \le 7$). The number of steps is at most $n / 9 + 9 \times \text{number of tens}$, taking at most a few hundred thousand steps for $n = 10^6$ and finishing in under 5 ms.
- Space: $O(1)$ auxiliary space.

## Code walkthrough
- `int steps = 0`: Step accumulator.
- `while (n > 0)`: Continues until the number is completely reduced to zero.
- `int temp = n, maxD = 0; while (temp > 0) { maxD = max(maxD, temp % 10); temp /= 10; }`: Scans digits of the current number to locate the maximum digit.
- `n -= maxD`: Subtracts the largest digit greedily.
- `steps++`: Records the completed subtraction move.

## Test it out loud
- Test case $1$: $n = 1 \implies \text{maxD} = 1, n$ becomes 0. Returns 1.
- Test case $2$: $n = 10 \implies 10 - 1 = 9 \to 9 - 9 = 0$. Returns 2.
- Test case $3$: $n = 15 \implies 15 - 5 = 10 \to 10 - 1 = 9 \to 9 - 9 = 0$. Returns 3.
- Test case $4$: $n = 23 \implies 23 - 3 = 20 \to 20 - 2 = 18 \to 18 - 8 = 10 \to 10 - 1 = 9 \to 0$. Returns 5.

## Follow-ups
- How to prove the greedy strategy is optimal? One can verify by mathematical induction that for any number $x$ in interval $[10k, 10k + 9]$, the sequence generated by greedy achieves $\text{dp}[x] = \min_{d} (\text{dp}[x - d] + 1)$.
- What if $n = 10^{18}$? Digit DP with state memoization or matrix transitions over powers of 10 solves $n$ up to $10^{18}$ in $O(\log n)$ time.

## Common mistakes
- Subtracting 0: For numbers like 20, the units digit is 0. Subtracting 0 results in an infinite loop. The digit chosen must be non-zero.
- Allocating a DP table of size $10^6$ when $O(1)$ space is sufficient: While DP is correct, simulating the greedy path directly uses zero extra memory.
